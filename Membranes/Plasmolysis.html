Before you watch: plasmolysis is osmosis happening to a plant cell

1. The setup: a plant cell is a solution inside a semi-permeable membrane. A plant cell's cytoplasm and its large central vacuole contain many dissolved solutes, each wrapped in a hydration shell of water molecules. The plasma membrane is the semi-permeable membrane: water can cross it, but most solutes can't. Outside the membrane sits the cell wall, which is rigid but fully permeable, so water and solutes pass through it freely. The cell wall doesn't decide where water goes; it only limits how much the cell can swell or shrink in shape. Normally the solution outside a plant cell has fewer solutes than the inside, so more free water is outside. Water moves in, the vacuole fills, and the cell pushes firmly against its wall. This pressure is called turgor pressure, and it is what keeps leaves and stems firm.

2. What happens in a salty (hypertonic) solution. Now place the cell in a solution with a higher solute concentration than the inside of the cell, such as salt water. The outside now has fewer free water molecules, because more of them are hogged by solutes. Free water still does what any molecule does: it moves down its concentration gradient, which now means out of the cell. The solutes inside can't leave to even things up, so the vacuole and cytoplasm lose water and shrink. Because the cell wall is rigid, it keeps its shape while the shrinking plasma membrane pulls away from the wall. This is plasmolysis. The cell loses turgor pressure, which is why plants wilt in salty soil. If the cell is moved back into a solution with fewer solutes than its interior, free water moves back in and the membrane presses against the wall again, as long as the cell hasn't been damaged.
